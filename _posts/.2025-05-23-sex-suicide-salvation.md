### Sex, Suicide, and Salvation

We’ve been lied to—by culture, by media, by friends who meant well but didn’t know better. Lied to about what sex is, what it does, and what it costs. We were told it’s just physical. Told it doesn’t matter. Told it’s casual.

But look around. Nothing about it is casual.

If it were, it wouldn’t end marriages. It wouldn’t justify revenge. It wouldn’t trigger trauma, heartbreak, obsession, custody battles, therapy, or violence. If sex were truly neutral, cheating wouldn’t matter—and yet it’s one of the only things nearly everyone agrees is unforgivable.

That alone should tell you something. We know, deep down, that sex bonds us. And when misused, it breaks us.

Even religion knew this long before psychology caught up. The Ten Commandments don’t just ban adultery—they ban lust. Not once, but twice. More attention than murder. That wasn’t an accident. That was an alarm bell. The spiritual traditions—Christian, Islamic, Jewish, even Eastern—all treat sexual betrayal as sacred violation. And it’s not because they’re prudes. It’s because they’re realists. Sex links souls, destabilizes hierarchies, and reshuffles destinies. Always has.

Science agrees—though it hates to say so out loud. Sex releases oxytocin and vasopressin, chemicals that form attachment and trust. For women, especially, the bond can be intensified by hormonal cycles and literal absorption of male DNA—in blood, in tissue, even in the brain. For men, it’s more territorial, more protective, but no less real. These aren’t just feels. They’re facts.

But we pretend otherwise. We call it empowerment. We call it exploration. We call it “just sex.”

Then why does it destroy people?

Why does it unravel relationships, ruin reputations, gut marriages, poison friend groups, and turn men and women into bitter shells of themselves?

In some parts of the world, if a man is found to be manipulated by a woman—if he’s lied to, betrayed, used for resources or status—he doesn’t just lose face. He loses everything. Respect. Rank. Promotion. In some circles, he’s considered unfit to lead. After all, how can you manage 100 people when you can’t even command honesty from one?

You can call that toxic masculinity if you want. Or you can call it what it is: a survival instinct. A man who can’t detect danger in the closest possible quarters is a danger to his entire tribe.

In business, it’s no different. Salesmen lose their edge, executives lose their focus, deals fall apart. I’ve seen it. The guy who was on top of the world gets blindsided by betrayal, and suddenly he’s not closing, not thinking straight, not himself. The ring he saved for gets spent on moving costs. The confidence he needed for the pitch gets drowned in doubt. And nobody mourns his fall. They just replace him.

There is no court for that. No lawsuit. No therapy app. No TED Talk.

Only the mirror. And maybe God.

And still—we laugh. We quote idioms like “get under one to get over another,” like it’s just harmless advice. But if you stop and listen, even that joke admits what culture won’t say out loud: sex breaks bonds. Sex means something. Or else it wouldn’t do anything at all.

You can’t hold both positions. Either it matters or it doesn’t. And if it doesn’t, then cheating shouldn’t hurt. But it does. If body count doesn’t affect bonding, then getting over someone shouldn’t require another body. But it does. The truth is sitting in plain sight, wrapped in jokes, buried in shame, ignored in policy—but alive in pain.

This is not about judgment. It’s about gravity.
This is not about policing women or excusing men.
It’s about waking up to the cost of forgetting what we already know.

So maybe, instead of pretending we’re above biology, above faith, above cause and effect—
We should start listening to what we’re actually saying.
And more importantly—what we’re living.
